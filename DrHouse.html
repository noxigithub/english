🦯 Diagnostic Differentials

Analyze the symptoms. Formulate a theory. Save the patient.

📋 Department Grammar Protocols

To present your differential diagnosis to the attending physician, you must use precise medical English:

⏳ Past Simple vs. Present Perfect

  • Past Simple: Completed actions in a finished time period. (e.g., "The patient fainted yesterday at 3 PM.")
  • Present Perfect: Actions that started in the past and continue now, or recent actions with present results. (e.g., "The patient has experienced chest pain for three days.")

🔮 Future: Will vs. Going To

  • Will: Spontaneous decisions or theories without hard evidence yet. (e.g., "I think it's lupus. I will order an ANA test.")
  • Going To: Predictions based on current, visible evidence. (e.g., "Look at that ECG drop. He is going to code!")

⚙️ Gerunds vs. Infinitives

  • Gerunds (-ing): After verbs like consider, suggest, avoid, deny. (e.g., "I suggest ordering a tox screen.")
  • Infinitives (to + verb): After verbs like decide, need, plan, refuse. (e.g., "We need to intubate immediately.")

Case #1: The Sweaty Executive

Male, 45, CEO

Presentation: The patient arrived at the ER with severe palpitations, profound sweating, and a throbbing headache. His ECG is normal (no ischemia), but his blood pressure is dangerously high (210/110). He thought he was having a heart attack.

Your Task: Write a short report.
  • Use Present Perfect to describe how long he has felt this way.
  • Use a Gerund to suggest a test (e.g., suggest ordering...).
  • Use Going To to state what will happen if his BP isn't lowered.
Attending's Hint: It looks like a cardiac event, but the normal ECG points to an endocrine issue. Have you considered a tumor on the adrenal glands that pumps out adrenaline? (Pheochromocytoma). Check his 24-hour urine metanephrines!

Case #2: The Blue Musician

Male, 25, Trumpet Player

Presentation: The patient's lips and fingers turned completely blue during a jazz gig. His oxygen saturation is reading 85%, but his lungs sound perfectly clear, and his chest X-ray is normal. When the nurse drew blood, it was chocolate brown.

Your Task: Write your theory.
  • Use Past Simple to ask/state what he did right before the gig.
  • Use an Infinitive to state what you need to do (e.g., need to administer...).
  • Use Will to predict his recovery.
Attending's Hint: The blood can't carry oxygen properly because the iron is oxidized. Did he use a topical anesthetic spray (like benzocaine) for a toothache before playing his trumpet? This is Methemoglobinemia. You need to give Methylene Blue.

Case #3: The Fainting Athlete

Female, 19, College Runner

Presentation: She collapsed in the middle of a marathon. She has no family history of heart disease, but her heart rate is currently erratic (arrhythmia). Her parents mention she had a "bad flu" two weeks ago but decided to run anyway.

Your Task: Plan the next steps.
  • Use Present Perfect to describe her recent illness.
  • Use a Gerund to talk about something she should avoid (e.g., avoid running...).
  • Use Will or Going To to explain her treatment plan.
Attending's Hint: Viruses can attack the heart muscle. A healthy athlete suddenly dropping with an arrhythmia after a viral infection? Suspect Viral Myocarditis. Order an echocardiogram and cardiac MRI.

Case #4: The Delirious Chef

Male, 30, Head Chef

Presentation: He came in complaining of severe abdominal pain. Surgery found nothing wrong. Now, he is hallucinating, having seizures, and his heart is racing (tachycardia). A nurse noticed that his urine sample, left near the sunny window, turned dark purple.

Your Task: Document the mystery.
  • Use Past Simple to state when the symptoms started.
  • Use an Infinitive to express a medical decision (e.g., decided to consult...).
  • Use Going To to predict what will happen if you give the wrong medication.
Attending's Hint: Abdominal pain, neuro/psych symptoms, and purple urine in sunlight? It's a classic metabolic disorder: Acute Intermittent Porphyria (AIP). Triggers include fasting (did he skip meals while cooking?) or certain drugs. Give IV Hemin.

Case #5: The Tired Traveler

Female, 50, Archaeologist

Presentation: She recently returned from a long excavation in rural South America. She presents with progressive fatigue, swelling in her legs (edema), and shortness of breath. Her ECG shows a Right Bundle Branch Block (RBBB) and signs of dilated cardiomyopathy.

Your Task: Write the final diagnosis.
  • Use Present Perfect to state where she has traveled.
  • Use a Gerund to consider a specific parasitic infection (e.g., consider treating...).
  • Use Will to propose a long-term care plan.
Attending's Hint: Heart failure + RBBB + travel to rural South America? Think of the Kissing Bug. This is Chagas Disease caused by Trypanosoma cruzi. The parasite has damaged her heart tissue.
